Probabilistic forecasts

This page shows how to get prediction intervals and predictive samples from ConformalSeasonalPool, how the method works, and how to score the result. It marks our own choices as ours. The code blocks are plain Julia: they are not run when the documentation is built. Their output was produced with Julia 1.12.5, and the random draws can differ on other Julia versions (see The draws and the random generator). Sources are listed under References.

Today the package gives probabilistic forecasts in one shape: Conformal Seasonal Pools (CSP; Manokhin 2026), around the seasonal naive forecast. It trains nothing. Intervals around other models are not available yet.

A worked example

A synthetic monthly series, eight years long, with a yearly cycle, a trend and some noise (no randomness is involved in building it):

using MachineLearningForecast, Dates, Statistics

t = collect(Date(2012, 1, 1):Month(1):Date(2019, 12, 1))
y = [50 + 10 * sin(2π * i / 12) + 0.2i + sin(i^2) for i in 1:96]
df = (ds = t, y = y)

ConformalSeasonalPool(12; freq=Month(1)) builds a Forecaster whose season is 12 steps long. fit trains nothing: it keeps each series' history. forecast with levels adds the interval columns:

csp = ConformalSeasonalPool(12; freq = Month(1))
fitted = fit(csp, df)
fcast = forecast(fitted, 12; levels = (80, 95))

keys(fcast)                          # (:ds, :y_hat, :lo_80, :hi_80, :lo_95, :hi_95)
fcast.ds[1:3]                        # 2020-01-01, 2020-02-01, 2020-03-01
round.(fcast.y_hat[1:3]; digits = 1) # [71.4, 76.5, 76.6]
round.(fcast.lo_95[1:3]; digits = 1) # [56.0, 60.5, 61.0]
round.(fcast.hi_95[1:3]; digits = 1) # [75.7, 80.5, 80.6]

y_hat is the seasonal naive forecast: each step repeats the value one season earlier. It is not the median of the draws. The interval columns come from 100 draws per step, which forecast_samples returns in long format, one row per step and draw:

draws = forecast_samples(fitted, 12)

keys(draws)                          # (:ds, :sample, :y_hat)
length(draws.y_hat)                  # 1200 = 12 steps * 100 draws
draws.sample[1:3]                    # [1, 2, 3]
round.(draws.y_hat[1:3]; digits = 1) # [72.4, 70.4, 73.7], draws 1 to 3 of step 1

backtest scores forecasts of held-out windows. The metrics crps, Coverage and IntervalWidth are scored on the fold's draws, and levels adds the interval columns to forecasts:

r = backtest(csp, df; horizon = 12, windows = 3, levels = (95,),
             metrics = (mae, crps, Coverage(95), IntervalWidth(95)))

keys(r.forecasts)   # (:fold, :origin, :step, :ds, :y, :y_hat, :lo_95, :hi_95)
s = summarize(r)
s.metric            # [:mae, :crps, :coverage_95, :interval_width_95]
round.(s.mean; digits = 2)   # [2.3, 1.7, 0.89, 16.45]
round.(s.std; digits = 2)    # [0.53, 0.29, 0.19, 2.6]

The three folds of 12 steps give 36 held-out values: far too few to judge coverage. They only show how the calls fit together.

How the method works

For one series y[1:T], season length m, and each horizon h = 1, ..., H, CSP draws B values (samples, 100 by default) from a mixture of two pools. The steps follow Algorithm 1 of the paper.

  1. The centre. mu_h is the seasonal naive forecast, y[T + h - m * ceil(h / m)]: the latest value at the same position in the season.
  2. The residual pool R. The signed seasonal differences y[t] - y[t - m] over the last calibration share (0.5) of the series, t > m. With 96 values and the default share, R holds 48 differences. Signed means a difference keeps its sign, so the pool can be asymmetric. It is one pool for all horizons.
  3. The seasonal pool S_h. The past values at the same position in the season as T + h, weighted by recency: the value at time t has weight exp(-recency * (T - t)) with recency = 0.01.
  4. The mixture weight w_h. With the default MachineLearningForecast.AdaptiveWeight: 0 when m = 1 (no seasonality), 0.3 when S_h holds one or two values, 0.5 from three on.
  5. Each draw. Draw u uniformly from (0, 1). If u < w_h, take a weighted draw from S_h. Otherwise take mu_h + r with r drawn uniformly from R. Repeat B times.

The newest value of S_h is the centre itself, so a share of the draws equals y_hat exactly. The intervals are read from the B draws of each step (see Levels and columns).

Some choices are ours, because the paper leaves them open:

  • The unit of recency. We count T - t in time steps of freq, so the weights fall by exp(-recency * m) per season (0.887 per season for m = 12). The paper gives the rate "in normalized time-index units" (p. 13), which is ambiguous. Per step is our reading. recency = 0 weighs the pool equally.
  • The sample quantile. See the next section.
  • The random generator and how series use it. See The draws and the random generator.

The paper's own choices are the defaults.

Levels and columns

A level is a percentage in (1, 100): levels=(80, 95). A fraction such as 0.95 is an ArgumentError that names the fix, so it is never read as a 0.95 % interval. A single number (levels=95) is allowed; repeating a level is an error.

forecast(fitted, 12; levels = 0.95)
# ArgumentError: an interval level is a percentage in (1, 100), got 0.95. Pass e.g. 95
# for a 95% interval, not 0.95.

forecast and backtest add lo_<L> and hi_<L> after y_hat, in the order the levels are given: lo_80, hi_80, lo_95, hi_95. A level with a decimal part uses an underscore: 99.5 gives lo_99_5 and hi_99_5. A Float32 level counts as it prints (Float32(99.9) is 99.9). A level that would give the name of an existing column (for example a time column called lo_95) is an error.

The interval is the central one: the sample quantiles of the step's B draws at (100 - L) / 200 and (100 + L) / 200. The quantile is Hyndman & Fan (1996) type 6, which is our choice (the paper names none). Type 6 puts the k-th smallest of B draws at probability k / (B + 1), so, for draws without ties, a new draw from the same distribution falls below it with that probability on average. For 100 draws and L = 95 the bounds are at positions 2.525 and 98.475 of the sorted draws. A level above 100 (B - 1) / (B + 1), about 98 for 100 draws, gives the smallest and largest draw:

f84 = fit(csp, (ds = t[1:84], y = y[1:84]))
fc99 = forecast(f84, 12; levels = 99)
keys(fc99)                  # (:ds, :y_hat, :lo_99, :hi_99)

Here fc99.lo_99 equals the minimum of the step's 100 draws, and fc99.hi_99 the maximum.

The draws and the random generator

ConformalSeasonalPool and PoolIntervals take rng=Random.Xoshiro(42). The seed 42 is the paper's, but our draws are not the paper's random stream. The method copies the generator when it is built, and again for every series at every call. So:

  • repeated calls give the same draws, and the generator you passed in, or the method's own, is never advanced;
  • the draws are reproducible on the same Julia version. For draws that also reproduce across versions, pass a StableRNGs.StableRNG;
  • a series' draws do not depend on which other series are fitted with it, or on the order of the rows.
forecast_samples(f84, 12).y_hat == forecast_samples(f84, 12).y_hat   # true

Because every series (and every backtest fold) starts from its own copy of the same generator, they start from the same random numbers on different histories. With the default Xoshiro the numbers almost always stay aligned; with a StableRNG they do not, because a draw from the residual pool can use more than one number, how many depending on the pool's size. Sharing a generator has two consequences:

  • the draws of different series are dependent. Never combine them by sample number, for example to sum a panel: the sum of the first draw of each series is not a draw of the sum, and its spread is wrong;
  • with the default Xoshiro, the Monte Carlo errors of the folds are correlated, so they do not average out over folds as they would with independent draws. To see their size, run the backtest with several seeds:
using Random
for seed in (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 42)
    c = ConformalSeasonalPool(12; freq = Month(1), rng = Xoshiro(seed))
    rs = backtest(c, df; horizon = 12, windows = 3, metrics = (Coverage(95),))
    println(seed, " ", round(only(summarize(rs).mean); digits = 3))
end
# 1 0.917, 2 0.889, 3 0.917, 4 0.917, 5 0.917, 42 0.889

Horizons are drawn independently. The draws of step 1 and of step 2 with the same sample number are not parts of one path, so the samples cannot give the distribution of a sum over steps. The paper states the same limit (p. 12).

Scores

All of these take the actuals y and a matrix S whose row i holds the draws for y[i]. The long table of forecast_samples lists the draws of a step one after the other, so a matrix with a row per step is a reshape and a transpose. Here the first 84 months are the training data and the last 12 months are held out:

actual = y[85:96]
d = forecast_samples(f84, 12)
S = permutedims(reshape(d.y_hat, 100, 12))   # 12 rows (steps), 100 columns (draws)

round(crps(actual, S); digits = 2)             # 1.67
round(fair_crps(actual, S); digits = 2)        # 1.64
round(pinball_loss(actual, S); digits = 2)     # 0.94
Coverage(95)(actual, S)                        # 1.0
round(IntervalWidth(95)(actual, S); digits = 1) # 19.2
  • crps is the CRPS (Gneiting & Raftery 2007) of the empirical distribution of the draws, averaged over the rows. This plug-in form scores the B draws as issued, so it is the one for verifying the forecast you give out (Zamo & Naveau 2018).
  • fair_crps uses the spread term over the B (B - 1) pairs of distinct draws (Ferro, Richardson & Weigel 2008). Use it to compare forecasts with different numbers of draws, or to score the distribution the draws come from. It needs B >= 2. The plug-in crps is higher by about E|X - X'| / (2B).
  • pinball_loss is the plain mean of the pinball loss over the quantiles 0.1, 0.2, ..., 0.9, the quantiles the paper reports (p. 8). It is not normalised: that is our choice.
  • Coverage is the share of actuals inside the central interval. A calibrated forecast scores near level / 100; higher is not better.
  • IntervalWidth is the mean width of the same interval. Compare widths only between forecasts with similar coverage (Gneiting & Katzfuss 2014: calibration first, then sharpness).

Lower is better for crps, fair_crps and pinball_loss; a lower IntervalWidth is better only at similar coverage. These are the same numbers backtest computes. The crps above equals the one of the last fold in r.scores, because that fold trains on the same 84 months and draws from the same copy of the generator.

In backtest, each sample score is computed on a fold's draws, pooled over all series of the fold. tune refuses these metrics. summarize reports the unweighted mean of the per-fold values. For Coverage and IntervalWidth that is not the same as the share or the mean over all rows of forecasts when folds have different numbers of rows, as in a ragged panel (next section). The pooled form is:

f = r.forecasts
mean(f.lo_95 .<= f.y .<= f.hi_95)    # share of all rows inside the interval
mean(f.hi_95 .- f.lo_95)             # mean width over all rows

For the single series above both agree with summarize, since each fold has 12 rows.

Panels

With id, every series has its own pools and its own centre; no model is shared (the seasonal naive model has nothing to learn). Series may differ in length:

yb = y .+ 100
yc = [20 + 5 * cos(2π * i / 12) + 0.1i + cos(i^2) for i in 1:60]
panel = (id = vcat(fill("A", 96), fill("B", 96), fill("C", 60)),
         ds = vcat(t, t, t[1:60]), y = vcat(y, yb, yc))

cp = ConformalSeasonalPool(12; freq = Month(1), id = :id)
fp = fit(cp, panel)

out = forecast(fp, 12; levels = 95)
keys(out)                       # (:id, :ds, :y_hat, :lo_95, :hi_95)
length(out.y_hat)               # 36 = 3 series * 12 steps
dp = forecast_samples(fp, 12)
keys(dp)                        # (:id, :ds, :sample, :y_hat)
length(dp.y_hat)                # 3600 = 3 series * 12 steps * 100 draws

Series B is series A plus 100, and both have 96 values. They use the same random numbers, so their draws differ by 100, sample by sample (up to rounding). Series A also gets the draws it gets when fitted alone:

a = dp.y_hat[dp.id .== "A"]
b = dp.y_hat[dp.id .== "B"]
b ≈ a .+ 100                                  # true
a == forecast_samples(fitted, 12).y_hat       # true

This is the dependence described above. Do not sum a and b by sample number.

A panel backtest pools the sample scores over all series of a fold, as it does for an unscaled point metric, so the series with large values dominate. If you need one score per series, coverage and width follow from forecasts (with f below, g = f.id .== "C"; mean(f.lo_95[g] .<= f.y[g] .<= f.hi_95[g])). A series backtested alone with the same fold origins gets the same draws as in the panel. With initial the folds are cut on the global time grid. Series C ends 36 months before the others, so it has fewer actuals in the folds:

rp = backtest(cp, panel; horizon = 12, initial = 54, levels = 95,
              metrics = (Coverage(95), IntervalWidth(95)))
f = rp.forecasts
[count(==(k), f.fold) for k in unique(f.fold)]   # [30, 24, 24] rows per fold
round.(summarize(rp).mean; digits = 3)           # [0.883, 14.739]
round(mean(f.lo_95 .<= f.y .<= f.hi_95); digits = 3)   # 0.885
round(mean(f.hi_95 .- f.lo_95); digits = 2)            # 14.48

The unweighted mean of the fold values (0.883 and 14.739) differs from the pooled values (0.885 and 14.48).

Settings

KeywordDefaultMeaning
periodrequiredseason length in steps of freq; 1 for none
weightAdaptiveWeight()the seasonal-pool weight; FixedWeight(w) is a constant, FixedWeight(0.5) is the paper's CSP-Fixed
calibration0.5the share of each series' end that forms the residual pool, in (0, 1]
recency0.01decay per step of the seasonal-pool weights; 0 weighs equally
samples100draws per series and step
rngXoshiro(42)the generator, copied as described above

AdaptiveWeight and FixedWeight are public but not exported: using MachineLearningForecast: FixedWeight. The defaults are the paper's.

Choosing period. It is the number of steps of freq in one cycle: 12 for monthly data with a yearly cycle, 7 for daily data with a weekly cycle, 24 for hourly data with a daily cycle, 52 for weekly data with a yearly cycle. period = 1 means no seasonality: the centre is the last value, the residual pool holds first differences, and the seasonal pool is not used (w = 0 with the default weight). A series needs at least period + 1 rows; with fewer, fit throws an ArgumentError that asks for at least period + 1 rows. A panel skips such a series with a warning, so it gets no forecast and no intervals.

A series also needs enough values for the residual pool: with the default share, the pool holds min(floor(T / 2), T - m) differences (1 for T = m + 1, 8 for T = 20 and m = 12). If the share is so small that floor(calibration * T) is 0, the forecast is an ArgumentError that names the step and the fix:

cs = ConformalSeasonalPool(12; freq = Month(1), calibration = 0.01)
forecast(fit(cs, df), 3; levels = 95)
# ArgumentError: the residual pool for step 1 is empty: the last calibration=0.01 of the
# 96 training values hold no seasonal difference y[t] - y[t - 12]. Raise calibration or
# provide a longer series.

A Forecaster built by hand accepts the interval method (MachineLearningForecast.PoolIntervals) only in the setup ConformalSeasonalPool builds: a SeasonalNaive model, FeatureSet(Lag(period)), Recursive() and no target transform. Other setups are refused with an error that names what to change.

What is not promised

The method has no coverage guarantee. Its residual pool is motivated by split-conformal calibration (Papadopoulos et al. 2002), whose finite-sample guarantee needs exchangeable data; serially dependent or non-stationary series are not. The paper makes no formal coverage claim, for the residual pool alone or for the mixture, and reports coverage as an empirical property: a mean of 0.89 at the nominal 0.95 on its benchmark (Table 1, p. 7). That is the paper's result on its data, not ours.

Other limits:

  • The residual pool is uniform over the calibration window and is not scaled by the series, so the intervals may be too wide or too narrow where the spread changes. The paper names scale normalisation as an untested extension (p. 12).
  • The draws at steps h and h + m come from the same distribution, so the intervals repeat with the season and do not widen with the horizon. The error of a seasonal naive forecast usually grows with each season ahead (for a seasonal random walk, k seasons ahead it is a sum of k seasonal differences), so beyond the first season the intervals can be too narrow. With FixedWeight(0.0) the 95 % interval of the example series has the same width, about 3.5, at each of 24 steps (2000 draws).
  • The seasonal pool holds old values. On a trending series such as the example it pulls draws toward past levels and widens the interval: about 20 with the default weight, against about 3.5 with FixedWeight(0.0) (2000 draws). Whether that is right for your series is for a backtest to show.
  • The draws are not paths (see above).
  • The recency rate, the calibration share and the number of draws are the paper's values, and it ran no sensitivity analysis for them (p. 12). The weights were fixed before it saw test scores and not tuned per dataset (p. 13).
  • Coverage and sharpness depend on the series. Measure coverage on your data with Coverage(95) in a backtest, over many origins and series, and with several seeds.
